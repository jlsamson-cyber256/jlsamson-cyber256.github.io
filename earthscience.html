The tide is taking more than it gives back.

A look at coastal and marine degradation in the Philippines — what's happening beneath the surface, and who is already paying for it.

What's happening

Coastal and marine degradation, in plain terms

The Philippines' coastal and marine ecosystems are under sustained pressure from three directions at once: overfishing and illegal commercial fishing inside municipal waters, marine litter building up on shorelines and seabeds, and the loss of coral reefs and mangroves that once buffered the coast and sheltered marine life.

Government fisheries data shows this isn't a future risk — it's a decade-long trend. Both municipal and commercial fisheries production have been declining since a peak around 2010, even as detections of illegal commercial fishing inside municipal waters have risen since the pandemic.

Who feels it first

What this costs people and ecosystems

Fisherfolk

Municipal fishing is the livelihood backbone of most coastal communities. As catch volumes shrink and illegal commercial vessels encroach on municipal waters, small-scale fisherfolk absorb the loss first and most directly.

Food security

Fish remains a primary protein source for many Filipino households. A shrinking, less reliable catch puts pressure on coastal food supply and prices.

Local economies

Coastal tourism and fish trade depend on visibly healthy reefs and shorelines. Litter accumulation and reef degradation make both harder to sustain.

Marine ecosystems

Coral reefs and mangroves are nurseries for fish and natural storm buffers. Their degradation reduces biodiversity and leaves coastlines more exposed to erosion and storm surge.
